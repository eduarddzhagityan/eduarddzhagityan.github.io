Higher Rates, Harder Deals: What the Fed's Move Means for M&A

September 19, 2026

Originally published on LinkedIn. Read the LinkedIn article ↗

The Federal Reserve's September 16 increase in the federal funds target range to 3.75–4.00% raises an important question for dealmakers: which acquisitions still create value when financing remains expensive?

The larger issue is the expected duration of higher rates. The Fed's updated projections place the median policy rate at 4.1% at the end of both 2026 and 2027. These are conditional projections, but they challenge acquisition strategies built around early refinancing at lower rates.

Selective transactions in the United States

In the United States, higher borrowing costs reduce debt capacity and put pressure on returns, particularly in leveraged buyouts. Buyers may lower bids while sellers hold to earlier valuations, prolonging negotiations. Acquirers with strong cash generation and limited refinancing needs gain a relative advantage. Acquisition finance will also depend on credit spreads, loan terms, and how much tightening markets had already anticipated.

Dollar funding and cross-border M&A

Globally, the effect extends through dollar funding and exchange rates. Companies earning local-currency revenues while servicing unhedged dollar debt are particularly exposed. If the dollar strengthens, some overseas targets become cheaper for U.S. buyers, while American acquisitions become more expensive for foreign bidders. That outcome is possible, not automatic: it depends on relative monetary-policy paths, risk sentiment, and hedging costs.

Geopolitics changes the deal model

Geopolitics adds another layer. Disruption around Hormuz and the Red Sea puts energy costs and supply reliability directly into acquisition models. Tariff uncertainty and investment restrictions can also weaken the economics of combining operations across borders. Conversely, acquiring local production, secure suppliers, or critical technology may become more valuable despite expensive financing.

Banking M&A: resilience matters as much as scale

Banking M&A deserves particular caution. A merger can diversify funding and spread technology costs, yet it can also combine securities losses, concentrated credit exposures, and unstable deposits. A larger balance sheet does not automatically provide greater resilience.

The likely outcome is a more differentiated M&A market: greater pressure on transactions dependent on cheap debt, alongside opportunities for financially strong buyers and acquisitions that improve operational security. For boards, the decisive test is whether the combined business can withstand expensive refinancing, delayed synergies, and geopolitical disruption simultaneously. Closing the deal is only the first test; financing and integrating it through the next shock is the harder one.
